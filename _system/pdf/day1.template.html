Summary

Key takeaways

AI features run on the same crawl and index as classic Search. Whether Google reaches and fetches your pages still decides everything downstream, including AI answers.
  1. Good SEO is good "GEO".AI Overviews and AI Mode are built on the core ranking systems and grounded in the Search index. No separate discipline to buy.
  2. Expect traffic to move.Google said outright that long-held traffic patterns are likely to fluctuate. Growth is in brainstorming, "which" and planning queries.
  3. There is now a real AI opt-out.A Search Console setting removes a property from AI Overviews, AI Mode and AI in Discover without, Google says, affecting regular rankings.
  4. llms.txt does nothing for Google Search.Google's guide says such files "neither harm nor help"; Gary Illyes doesn't expect that to change.
  5. One crawling platform serves Google's products.Googlebot is one client of it. Search, Ads, Shopping and Images share it, and one hostload limit per host.
  6. Your server's health sets the crawl rate.Connect time, time to first byte and 429 or 5xx responses decide how hard Google crawls. Firewalls that silently block Googlebot look like a failing server.
  7. Login walls and paywalls read as 404.Pages that answer Google's crawlers with a 403 or a 402, both on the rise, are dropped. A CDN captcha served with 200 becomes a soft 404.
  8. A crawler obeys exactly one robots.txt group.Groups don't add up: Googlebot ignores the * group when a Googlebot group exists, the longest matching rule wins, and one stray line can merge or split groups.
  9. Crawl demand is inherited by path.With little data on a URL, Google uses the quality and popularity of its parent folder, then that folder's parent.
  10. noindex costs crawl; disallow doesn't.Google must fetch a page to read noindex. crawl-delay is ignored. Faceted URLs are the classic budget leak.
  11. AI Mode doesn't mean more Googlebot.AI Mode sits on the existing crawl infrastructure, and crawling more often doesn't make a page rank better.
  12. There is no duplicate content penalty.Google picks one URL to keep. The real cost is losing control of which one, and crawling every copy.

Section 04 · How Search works and where's AI?

How Search works, and where AI sits

"If you know how Search works, you largely also know how AI features work." AI Mode and AI Overviews use the same crawl and index as classic Search. They differ only at serving.

Stage Gary Illyes: AI Overviews and AI Mode may have extra processes of their own, like any search feature, but the bulk of their processing is the same as for normal Search.

The pipeline, step by step

StepWhat Cherry Prommawin explained Stage
DiscoveryThere are trillions of URLs, so many that even Google can't count them, and some will never be found (not in docs). Hub pages such as the homepage and categories may be visited more often, because they link to new pages.
CrawlingA crawler downloads a page, extracts its links and repeats; Googlebot is Search's main crawler.
IndexingThe HTML is parsed, JavaScript-heavy pages are rendered with the latest Chromium, duplicates are clustered under one canonical URL, and signals are stored for index selection and later for ranking.

ServingHappens in real time: the query is cleaned up, its language detected and expanded; quality is one of the most important ranking factors; then the results page is built from the features that fit the intent. A majority of search features now use AI (not in docs).

"Good SEO is good GEO"

Stage Gary Illyes argued that "GEO" is a new label invented to create a new field. The work doesn't need it: understand how SEO works and you are covered. He traced "SEO is dead" posts back to a forum thread called "Search engine R.I.P." from 10 November 1997; they still appear every month. SEO is not dead, he said: the AI features create more opportunities.

Slide "Don't worry about what to call it. Good SEO is good GEO, AEO." Three reasons: generative AI features are built on the core ranking systems; query fan-out expands the original query to find related information; and generative AI features highlight content indexed by Google Search.

Docs Google's optimisation guide for generative AI (updated 10 July 2026) agrees: optimising for generative AI search "is optimizing for the search experience, and thus still SEO". It describes query fan-out as running several related searches at once — a question about lawn weeds also searches herbicides and prevention. Stage Gary Illyes called fan-out nothing new: "just ten different searches that fire in the background", each a normal search on the same systems.

Section 05 · Cherry Prommawin and Gary Illyes

How crawling works

Google runs one crawling system for all its products, and its first job is not to harm your server. That is why speed and errors on your side decide how much Google crawls.
  • Slide The crawler has "multiple tasks": fetch from the internet, "ensure we don't break the internet", and enforce robots.txt policies. Fetched data goes on to indexing.
  • Slide The scheduler is "shared scheduler infrastructure". It decides what to fetch and when; each team sets the scheduling parameters for its own user agent.
  • Slide Search, Ads, Shopping and Images all request through one "centralized crawling infrastructure". Its primary mandate: fetch "while strictly preventing the overloading of external servers".
  • Slide Discovery runs through links: a news homepage links to /sport and /tech, which link to their articles.

Googlebot is just an HTTP client

Stage Gary Illyes: "Googlebot is just a client. It is an HTTP client. There's nothing all that much special about it." Like a browser, it fetches the URL it was given and returns the bytes. Google's crawling is "a large-scale distributed swarm of simple HTTP clients", and AI agents are technically the same thing: HTTP clients acting for a user or a service (not in docs).

Stage Google runs probably hundreds, if not thousands, of crawlers (not in docs), some named and some not, all on one infrastructure that enforces the same crawling policies. Googlebot is the crawler for web search, AI features included. Docs Google's Inside Googlebot post speaks of dozens of other clients, with only the larger ones documented. Analysis A Google user agent missing from the public lists is not proof of a fake: verify by reverse DNS or Google's published IP ranges.

Docs Googlebot fetches only the first 2MB of each URL, HTTP headers included (64MB for PDFs). Anything past the cutoff is not fetched, rendered or indexed, so keep the title, canonical and key structured data high in the HTML.

How the scheduler decides

  • Stage Google finds URLs mainly in pages it has crawled, and also in sitemaps: a format from around 2005, "nothing fancy", that Google still uses.
  • Stage The scheduler logs how often each page changes and crawls fast-changing pages first: a news homepage before a terms of service page that changes once a year.
  • Stage URLs or sites known to be historically spammy are very likely deprioritised.
  • Stage Each team sets its own priorities. Search cares a lot about quality; Ads checks every publisher page that wants to show ads, as the URLs come in. Crawling for Gemini may care more about amount, because for language models "the number of tokens is actually more important" than the quality (not in docs).

Stage To debug crawling, Gary Illyes called Search Console's Crawl Stats report (Settings → Crawl stats) the most valuable tool: how much Google crawls, the errors it got and the content types each crawler fetched. Server logs are "the real thing", for those who can read them.

Analysis · two consequences

Orphan pages are slow pages. A URL with no internal links depends on sitemaps alone to be found, so discovery is slow and demand stays low.

Every Google fetch counts against the same host. Ads and Shopping fetches share host capacity with Googlebot. A merchant feed or ad landing pages hitting a slow server can reduce how much is crawled for Search.

Section 06 · How crawling errors affect Search

Crawl errors: what Google sees when it can't reach you

The most damaging crawl errors are the ones site owners never see. A firewall that blocks Googlebot shows up on Google's side only as failed connections — and Google reacts by crawling less.

Stage Gary Illyes: DNS problems and firewalls are blind spots. Google doesn't know a firewall is the cause, it only sees requests fail. He called soft 404s one of the biggest problems on the internet right now for crawling and for showing up in Search.

Status codes, class by class

ClassWhat Cherry Prommawin explained Stage
1xx InformationalOnly says the request arrived and more is coming; no meaning of its own for crawling (not in docs).
2xx Success200: what comes back is eligible for indexing — though, as Gary Illyes put it, 200 only means the server believes it did what was asked. 204 No Content: nothing to index.
3xx RedirectionFollowed to the new location, permanent (301, 308) or temporary (302, 307); indexing depends on what the target returns. 304: unchanged since the last visit.
4xx Client error404 and 410: nothing here, not indexable. 403: no permission, so nothing to index. 429 is the exception: it means "slow down", and Google does.
5xx Server errorCrawling slows, because the server, and often the whole site, can't serve requests, and Google doesn't want to break it.

Docs Google's crawlers follow up to 10 redirect hops by default; content served by the redirecting URL is ignored and only the final target is processed.

Login walls, paywalls and pay-per-crawl

Stage Gary Illyes: Google has recently seen more 403 responses to its crawlers, which he described as "authentication required". It treats them as client errors, technically like 404 or 410, and drops the pages from Search and its AI features. 402 Payment Required is no different, because Googlebot can't pay: "402 will just mean 404 to us". The uptick in 402s is even more recent than the one in 403s (not in docs).

Analysis

Never answer verified Googlebot with 401, 402 or 403 from a login wall, a paywall or a pay-per-crawl setup: the pages leave the index. Google's status code page also says not to use 401 or 403 to limit crawling. To slow Google down for a while, return 429 or 503.

DNS, networks and CDNs

  • Stage Gary Illyes called DNS and network errors very common, and DNS problems very pesky to debug. Both can get a site removed from Search and, with it, from every feature that depends on Search; DNS errors do so very aggressively. Search Console alerts you.
  • Stage Most network errors happen between your origin and Google's data centres, invisible to both sides (not in docs). Timeouts usually start close to the site: a firewall, the CDN, the host or the DNS provider.
  • Stage A rise in HTTP errors can be a CDN throttling crawlers by injecting 429 or 503 responses on the way between Google and your site. CDN captcha challenges often return 200; Googlebot doesn't solve them, so indexing classes the page as a soft 404. Analysis Day 2 added that such pages can also be clustered as duplicates. Either way, the real pages drop out.
  • Stage What to do first: ask the host or CDN whether they changed anything, check for new firewall rules, then ask in the CDN's forum. Google can't see these errors, so it can't help with them.

HTTP/3

Stage From my notes, session not recorded: Googlebot does not support HTTP/3 today. Docs Consistent: Google's crawlers use HTTP/1.1 and HTTP/2, pick whichever crawls best, and may switch between sessions. HTTP/2 can save server resources but gives no ranking benefit.

Analysis

This doesn't mean "don't use HTTP/3" — browsers benefit from it, and servers offer it alongside HTTP/2 and HTTP/1.1. The rule is: never run a host that only works over HTTP/3, and make sure CDN or firewall rules written for HTTP/3 traffic don't break the protocols Googlebot uses.

How to find these errors

  1. Search Console → Settings → Crawl stats → Host status: robots.txt fetch, DNS resolution and server connectivity. Any red in the last 90 days deserves a ticket.
  2. In the same report, break requests down by response. A rising share of 5xx or 429 means Google is being told to slow down.
  3. Ask whoever runs the CDN or WAF for blocks on Google's published crawler IP ranges. Verify Googlebot by reverse DNS, never by user agent alone.
  4. For temporary blocks, return 503 or 429 — never a 200 page with a captcha or error message, which becomes a soft 404 and can replace your real content in the index.
  5. Review Soft 404 in the page indexing report, where Gary Illyes said to look them up; Crawl Stats is for the other crawl issues. On local and service sites, thin location pages with little unique content are the usual cause.

Section 07 · How Google interprets robots.txt · Lightning session B

robots.txt, read the way Google reads it

robots.txt has only three rules, yet most mistakes come from three things people get wrong: a crawler obeys only one group, the longest matching path wins, and $ anchors the whole URL — parameters included.

What robots.txt is, and what it isn't

Stage Google's talk started in 1994, when bots were crashing servers and Martijn Koster proposed a text file at the root of a site with rules for automated clients. Google supported it from its first crawl in 1996, as a Stanford PhD project. Today it is an IETF standard, RFC 9309, officially the Robots Exclusion Protocol, and basically all major crawlers follow it; Google does because anyone who wants to opt out of crawling should be able to. It controls which clients may access what. It has nothing to do with how the content is used.

Not a security measure
Stage The file sits at a predictable public address, so anyone can read the paths it disallows. Protect a secret folder with authentication, or keep it off the internet.
Three rules, one place
Stage Always at the root of the host. Only user-agent (a named crawler, or * for all), disallow and allow. Everything is allowed by default, so allow only re-opens a path inside a disallowed one.
Comments
Stage # starts a comment, and a line a parser can't read is skipped anyway, as the standard requires. To show why comments help, the talk pointed to the file of thebestfriedchickenever.com: mostly an ASCII-art drawing in comment lines (not in docs).

Stage Google called the format extremely forgiving: a typo in a path only blocks the wrong path, a typo in a rule name makes Google ignore that line, and the rest of the file still counts (not in docs). Analysis Google's open-source parser even accepts common misspellings of disallow and user-agent, though not of allow. Other crawlers may be stricter: spell rule names correctly.

The puzzle from the slide

# Let's craft a robots.txt!

user-agent: *
disallow: /
allow: /$

user-agent: bingbot
user-agent: googlebot
disallow: /
allow: /$
allow: /politics/
allow: /*/live/

user-agent: google-extended
disallow: /subscriptions/
allow: /

Docs The rules that decide it:

  1. One group per crawler. Several user-agent lines can share a group. A crawler follows only the most specific group naming it — so Googlebot ignores the * group here entirely.
  2. Longest path wins. Within the group, the rule with the longest matching path applies.
  3. Ties go to allow. If an allow and a disallow match equally, the less restrictive wins.
  4. * and $. * matches any characters; $ marks the end of the URL.

Stage The talk's own walk-through used the same tools the other way round: disallow: /*/live/ blocks /science/live/ and /sports/live/, and allow: /science/live/ re-opens that one path. user-agent: *, disallow: / and allow: /$ let unnamed crawlers fetch only the homepage: /$ means only the root, /cats$ exactly /cats.

Groups: one per crawler, never added together

Stage A user-agent line with its rules forms a group, and one group can name several crawlers, such as Googlebot and Bingbot. Give a crawler its own group only when it needs rules the * group shouldn't grant everyone. To give one crawler an extra rule on top of another group's rules, add one more group for it: Google's spec merges all groups that name the same user agent.

# The quiz answer
user-agent: googlebot
disallow: /staging/
allow: /staging/preview/

Stage The talk ended with a quiz. Its answer here: one Googlebot group holding both rules, not an allow in the * group and not a Googlebot group with only the allow.

Docs Why: a crawler follows only the most specific group naming it. An allow in the * group opens the path to every crawler; a Googlebot group with only the allow frees Googlebot from the * group's disallow: /staging/, so all of /staging/ opens to it.

The unknown-line trap

Stage The real trouble, Google's talk said, is an unknown line between two user-agent lines. Should the first crawler inherit the rules that follow? While writing RFC 9309, Google asked people, and opinions split roughly 50-50 (not in docs). How the question was settled is unclear in the recording.

Slide A later slide of Google's talk showed how two crawlers read such a file: "Different crawlers behave differently! … Unpredictability is never a good time."

Analysis

Content-Signal lines are now common because a large CDN provider adds them to its managed robots.txt files. Google supports only user-agent, allow, disallow and sitemap and ignores the rest. Never place any other directive between user-agent lines: put it after a group's rules, and test the file with each search engine's tools (Bing's robots.txt tester, Google's open-source robots.txt parser).

Google-Extended, crawl-delay and llms.txt

Docs Google-Extended is a control token, not a crawler. It decides whether content may train future Gemini models and ground Gemini apps and Vertex AI, and it "does not impact a site's inclusion in Google Search nor is it used as a ranking signal".

Stage The talk: disallow a directory or the whole site for Google-Extended and Google keeps it out of Gemini model training, and respects that. Other companies have similar tokens, such as Apple's Applebot-Extended (that part not in docs). The speaker believed Google was the first to offer such an opt-out. Analysis Not quite: OpenAI documented how to block GPTBot in August 2023, before Google-Extended arrived on 28 September 2023. What was new was a token that separates AI-training use from Search for pages Google's existing crawlers already fetch.

crawl-delay
Docs Not supported by Googlebot; silently ignored. robots.txt is cached for up to 24 hours, and only the first 500 KiB is read.
llms.txt
Slide "LLMS.txt isn't necessary." Stage Speakers at the event were openly dismissive. Docs Such files "neither harm nor help" — Google Search ignores them.

The robots.txt report

Stage Search Console's robots.txt report shows the file as Google last fetched it, with a version history and the errors and successes of each fetch. It uses the parser Google open-sourced at github.com/google/robotstxt. Use it to catch CDNs that change your robots.txt without telling you, and hosts that cloak the file, which happens more often than people think (the last two points not in docs).

Docs The report covers the top 20 hosts of a Domain property, with fetch status, warnings and errors. To test one URL, Google points to the URL Inspection tool and its open-source robots.txt library.

Lightning session B: when a redirect meets a blocked URL

Stage Dave Smart opened Lightning session B with the pitfalls he sees most. First: groups are not additive. If the * group blocks /goats/ and /cows/ and you add a Googlebot group that blocks /dogs/, only that group applies to Googlebot, so it is free to crawl the goats and the cows.

  • Stage robots.txt is checked for every URL in a redirect chain, and crawling stops at the first blocked one (not in docs). Search Console reports the block only on the first URL, which is confusing: the URL shown as blocked isn't disallowed itself.
  • Stage It applies to every kind of redirect, not only JavaScript: a hop through an external authorisation service blocked by its own robots.txt, content that moved through several URLs over the years until one of them got blocked, or a redirect served only to Googlebot's user agent (not in docs).
  • Stage So when Search Console says a URL is blocked by robots.txt and the file doesn't block it, check whether the URL redirects, and test every URL in the chain.

Section 08 · Cherry Prommawin, Search Relations

Crawl budget

Crawl budget is two separate limits: how much your server can take, and how much Google wants. Speed raises the first; quality and popularity raise the second. Most sites only ever hit the second.

Stage In one line: crawl budget is the attention span Google gives a website — and better performance increases it. More formally, it is the finite amount of crawling resources Google gives a site, which decides how many of its pages are found and how often they are revisited.

  • Stage Hostload is a proxy for how many requests per second the connection can take. It applies per host, not per site: your CDN, app, subdomains and www host may each be a different host.
  • Stage The quality behind crawl demand is the quality of the site as a whole, not of one page (not in docs). Analysis Read it as site quality setting the baseline; Google's guide still lists page-level quality among the demand factors.

Quality is inherited by path

Slide If quality or popularity is unknown, the parent root's aggregate quality or popularity is used, then that path's parent's, and so on (not in docs).

Analysis

Put new high-value content where Google already trusts you. A new folder under a section Google rates poorly starts with that section's low demand.

Who needs to care

Docs Google's crawl budget guide is written for sites with 1 million+ unique pages changing weekly, 10,000+ pages changing daily, or many URLs stuck in "Discovered – currently not indexed". Analysis On a smaller site, slow indexing is almost always a demand problem (quality), not a capacity problem.

Stage Asked whether crawl budget is still a priority in 2026: not every site needs to worry, and that has always been so. A site with fewer than a few thousand URLs likely has no problem, and a larger one doesn't necessarily have one either. Analysis Between that and the guide's million-page threshold, check Crawl Stats before blaming crawl budget for slow indexing.

What burns it: the six faceted-URL traps

Slide Server errors unrelated to load, useless pages and resources, and infinite URL spaces. Faceted navigation was the worked example. Stage URLs that differ are different URLs to Google, crawled even when they lead to the same content: calendars and endless page versions burn budget.

Docs Google's faceted navigation guide prefers prevention: block filter URLs in robots.txt and keep crawlable only item pages plus one unfiltered listing page — or use URL fragments, which Google generally doesn't crawl. rel="canonical" and nofollow are weaker, slower options.

How to manage it

01
Use HTTP caching. Serve 304 for unchanged pages; ETag preferred.
02
Have good navigation. Clear internal links for discovery and priority.
03
Restrict faceted and action URLs — add to cart, wishlist, print, sort.
04
Improve or remove useless content: low-quality, spam, duplicate and soft-error pages. Return 404/410 for removed pages.

Stage Status codes don't all cost the same. Every 2xx fetch consumes crawl budget; 4xx responses don't affect it, because pages come and go on the web. Analysis A 404 is still a fetch, so read the point this way: a 4xx doesn't slow crawling, and Google crawls a 404 URL less often over time. 429 is the exception and slows crawling like a 5xx.

Questions sent in before the event

Does AI Mode mean more crawling? Stage No: AI Mode sits on top of Google's existing crawling infrastructure, so Google doesn't crawl a site more because of it. Expect more crawling overall, though, from the many other services, AI included, that now crawl the web.

Does crawl frequency affect rankings? Stage "If your crawl rate is increased, that doesn't mean that you would rank better." Frequent crawling is a consequence, not a cause: it tends to signal interest in the site and good content.

How do I check? Stage In Crawl Stats, by response and by file type: most crawling going to images on a site with no images worth crawling is an anomaly. It also separates discovery (new URLs) from refresh (known URLs) and drills down to the problem URLs. Look now and then; don't obsess.

Lightning talks from the community

Google picked these seven-minute talks from close to 200 submissions, because the SEO community holds knowledge worth sharing. They are community views, not Google's: the labels show what Google's documentation backs.

Session A: automation and AI

AI that applies your rules
James Powley
Stage Break a deliverable into steps and use AI only where it adds scale; people set the strategy and the scoring. A competitor keyword analysis (26 competitors, 79 data points per keyword) fell from three days to a few hours, and a 3,000-post blog audit came back grouped, flagged for cannibalisation and prioritised. On the AI: "it didn't decide what good looked like. We did that."
LLM errors on search data
Rafael Kovashikawa
Stage A chatbot called an average position moving from 8 to 9 an improvement. His rules: do "not let the model grade its own homework"; every figure needs a receipt, traceable to the data request ("If there is no receipt, there is no reimbursement."); give the model your metric definitions as fixed context. Polished prose is no proof of correct analysis: borrowing Pascal's line on makers of false windows, he said LLMs sometimes don't aim for the most accurate figures. Docs In Search Console, position 1 is the top.
An LLM fix loop for structured data
A community demo
Stage A script runs Google's Rich Results Test, by URL for public pages and by pasted code for pages Google can't fetch, gives a large model the markup, the errors and Google's documentation, and re-tests the new JSON-LD, with at most three attempts. A small, cheap model picked at most two keywords per page, never brand or navigation terms. Errors on the test page included an empty name and a price of zero; warnings were treated as not critical (a largely unintelligible recording). Docs Only critical issues stop a rich result. Code mode still needs the page's resources reachable without a login, and merchant listings need a name and a price above zero. Analysis A passing test proves the JSON-LD is valid, not that its values are true.
Automating monthly reports
Kira Breuer
Stage Monthly reports took each SEO manager a full workday, and nobody read them. A non-developer automated them with Python and AI coding help: data in, a script, an AI summary, a dashboard. Start tiny, read the code the AI writes: "you don't need to be an expert; you just need to try." Docs The Search Console API is free, with usage limits, and needs OAuth2 credentials.

Preparing sites for humans and AI agents · Carlos Ortega

Stage The web is now visited by people and AI agents, and agents understand a page through a screenshot, the DOM and the accessibility tree. Docs Google's guide for generative AI features describes browser agents the same way and recommends semantic HTML.

  • Stage ARIA is a set of attributes, not a language: a div used as an "add to favourites" button gets role="button", an aria-label and aria-pressed. No ARIA is better than bad ARIA.
  • Stage WebMCP lets a site declare actions for agents instead of being scraped; not yet in all browsers, but testable through browser flags. Docs Chrome describes it as a proposed web standard, testable in an origin trial from Chrome 149 or behind a flag.
  • Stage Don't publish markdown copies of pages for agents: they are duplicates (he also called them a possible source of cloaking, an uncertain word in the recordings), and models read HTML, CSS and JavaScript anyway. llms.txt is not necessary: in a summer 2026 study, heard as Ahrefs', 97% of almost 40,000 sites had no AI agent hits on the file. Analysis Ahrefs' published study fits: about 38,000 of 137,210 domains had the file, and 97% of those files got no requests in May 2026; the rest got about 20 each, not "two hits a month".
  • Stage Schema markup tells an agent which number is the price (not in docs). Analysis Google says structured data is not required for generative AI search; use it for rich results and clear data, not as an AI lever.
  • Docs Headings out of order don't matter to Google Search. Analysis Keep a logical hierarchy anyway, for screen readers and agents that read the accessibility tree.

GEO: its numbers and its myths · Thiago Pojda

  • Stage SEO turned everything into a metric because "an idea doesn't get you budget approval; a number does". Not all of it was wrong: a site still has to be crawlable and visible, and much of that work is plumbing, not gaming.
  • Stage A study found sites with JSON-LD shown much more often, and the industry added schema everywhere; a later controlled study that only added markup found a negative correlation. The markup marked teams doing everything else right (not in docs).
  • Stage Listicles earn more AI mentions because LLMs mostly check that a thing exists, not that the content is honest; the industry is overusing them as it once overused links.
  • Stage A third-party study, heard as Peec AI's: 43% of prompts produced fan-out queries in another language than the user's (not in docs). A Spanish prompt about places to eat in Barcelona fanned out entirely in English. Analysis Peec AI's published analysis (February 2026, over 10 million ChatGPT prompts) puts it differently: 43% of the fan-out searches for non-English prompts ran in English, and nearly 78% of non-English prompt runs had at least one English fan-out. The 43% is a share of fan-outs, not of prompts.
  • Stage The speaker argued for adopting the GEO label as a fresh start for the industry — the opposite of Google's view that morning.

Session C: crawling

Similar URLs
Tobias Schwarz
Stage URLs that differ in a few characters point to duplicates, broken links and needless redirects; he found five kinds on well-known brand sites (table below). To find them, normalise the URL list, build a "uniform" URL (one scheme, lowercase, no www or index.html, sorted parameters) and sort by it. To prevent them, compute the expected URL for every request and redirect when it differs; test IDs with leading zeros too. Analysis Prefer the redirect to an error page: it passes signals to the canonical.
Technical SEO in AI search
Jovana Avramovic
Stage More than half of internet traffic in 2025 was non-human, with no source named (not in docs). Search now happens everywhere and in conversation, and AI turns one query into many, in other languages too; technical SEO stays the foundation. Models, like people, remember the start and end of a list best: "It's relevant to put your most important information at the beginning or at the end." Analysis A community heuristic, not something Google said; Google advises writing for people without chopping content. Her summary: "The target is not shifting; it is expanding."

Section 10 · Q&A · John Mueller and Gary Illyes

The Q&A: crawling questions, answered

Questions from the room and from registration, paraphrased, now checked against two audio recordings of most of the panel. An answer names a speaker only where the recording shows who gave it; otherwise it says "a panelist" or "Google".
Question
How does crawling for AI differ from crawling for Search?
Stage A panelist: "my feeling is a lot of the AI crawlers are still a bit stupid." In logs, search crawlers follow what changes and what is valuable; many AI crawlers work through a site in order and take everything. Training mainly needs a very large number of tokens, wherever they come from (not in docs). Can optimising for AI hurt classic search? Yes, a panelist said, pointing to questionable GEO advice online.
Question
Does llms.txt matter? PageSpeed Insights and Lighthouse check for it.
Stage Gary Illyes: not for Google Search, and he doesn't expect it to, though he has been wrong before; it matters to some people, hence the Lighthouse check. A panelist: "Just because maybe something will change in the future doesn't mean you should take action now." Most such files come from an SEO plugin checkbox, and "it's designed for allegedly intelligent systems" that should be able to parse a website. And cats.txt, a satire, gets more requests than llms.txt in some logs: crawlers fetch whatever they find. Docs Lighthouse marks its llms.txt audit not applicable when the file is missing.
Question
How are huge real-time sites, such as sports sites, crawled?
Stage With no special handling. When URLs change often or are useful to users, Google raises crawl demand and tries to raise its crawl capacity for the site, the same logic as for small sites. If the server can't cope, Google backs off again. And demand isn't only Search's: when Search, Google Ads or another product wants enough of a site's URLs, Google raises demand and crawls up to it, as far as capacity allows.
Question
How do I spot crawl waste in my logs?
Stage Logs alone can't show it, because they don't show where your crawl limit lies (not in docs). In Crawl Stats, check whether total crawling has plateaued and whether response times hold Googlebot back. Logs do pay off on sites with filters or complicated URLs: redirect parameter variants to the normalised URL, which costs crawl budget at first but leaves a clean slate. Many complaints turn out to be a plugin, such as a WordPress calendar adding calendar parameters to every page's URLs (not in docs); Google crawls them until large samples show they are useless. Such URLs can also be handled at the web server, with a rule or a module in Apache, for example.
Question
Is crawl budget slowing how fast new articles are found?
Stage Gary Illyes: news sites rarely need to worry; Google crawls them aggressively. Measure the time from publishing to Googlebot's first crawl: flat or falling is fine, a rising trend needs work, and for fresh stories something like two hours is probably not great (not in docs). Then look in the logs for URLs crawled again and again, and disallow sections you never want crawled, such as /ads. John Mueller: Crawl Stats' discovery-versus-refresh split gives a rough view; if about half the crawling goes to discovery, finding new pages is not the problem. Analysis Freed budget only moves on sites that hit their capacity limit.
Question
On a site of 100 million pages, is a crawl drop about demand or capacity?
Stage There is no easy way to tell, but most of the time a capacity-limit drop is an abrupt step down: hostload going from 10 to 5, say. Look at how many connections Googlebot opens to the site: if that number fell, the capacity limit changed. Analysis The panelist put the step as five requests per second; Google's guide counts the limit in parallel connections and how long they stay open, not in requests per second.
Question
What will Search Console report on AI features?
Stage A panelist expected more AI reporting, without a timeline or a promise. Google said the reports shouldn't simply mirror classic results, because people learn more before they click, so it first has to find which data is useful. Query data is hard: AI questions don't map back to keywords, and the data would have to be grouped to protect privacy.
Question
Will crawl stats come to the Search Console API?
Stage No plans known: it would be a new part of the API, while a variation of the Performance report would be easier. Search Relations now talks with the Search Console team about the API more than before: with so many vibe-coded tools, the easy answer is to use the API, and a gap in it is annoying. To get a feature built, ask for it publicly and in volume.
Question
How do I migrate without losing indexing?
Stage Probably not with sitemaps: decide what matters to the business first. Listing the new URLs can't hurt, but it isn't the main tool. Google merged 12 or 14 language blogs, a Help Center and its developer site into one. First it protected the popular URLs (not in docs); where two language versions duplicated each other, both old URLs got one target path. A fun fact: JavaScript was used, the only option the person doing it had (whether for the redirects themselves or for the mapping is not fully clear) (not in docs). Analysis That was the move to Google Search Central in November 2020, about six years before the event. Don't copy the JavaScript: Google sees such redirects only after rendering, and asks for server-side 301 or 308 redirects where possible. Stage And a sitemap has no sweet spot: list every URL you want indexed.
Question
Will Google offer separate AI user agents?
Stage Nearly all mainstream AI systems already use their own. A panelist doubted per-bot policies make sense yet, and called blocking all AI crawlers a personal choice. For Google's AI training, use Google-Extended. An unknown crawler in your logs: search its user agent for its token (not in docs). Default-deny is "a bad pattern, because you don't know what you're blocking." Analysis Google-Extended is a usage token, not a crawler: it doesn't stop Googlebot.
Question
What if paginated pages become one load-more page?
Stage Thousands of paginated category pages replaced by a JavaScript load-more button: should the old pages redirect to page 1? A panelist called pagination one of the trickiest things in web development, and the switch "kind of dangerous, depending on what you want to achieve": "Googlebot is not running around clicking on buttons". Without crawlable links to the further pages, Google won't see them at all. Docs Each page needs its own URL and a plain <a href> to the next; don't canonicalise to page 1; rel=next/prev is no longer used.
Question
How do I make crawlers behave, beyond robots.txt?
Stage Research, malware-scanning and privacy crawlers are a separate, generally useful category. Google is working on crawler best practices and offering them an exemption, apparently because they sometimes need to ignore robots.txt or probe URLs other crawlers wouldn't touch (a best reading of the recording) (not in docs). Analysis This matches a public IETF draft, "Crawler best practices": a draft, not Google documentation.

Section 12 · Take it back to the office

Day 1 audit checklist

Nineteen checks that turn Day 1 into work. Most take minutes in Search Console and one server log file.
Access and errors
  • Crawl stats → Host status shows no DNS, robots.txt or connectivity failures in the last 90 days
  • Crawl stats by response: the share of 5xx and 429 is flat or falling
  • CDN / WAF rules don't block Google's published crawler IP ranges; Googlebot verified by reverse DNS
  • Maintenance, rate limits and bot challenges return 503 or 429, never a 200 error or captcha page
  • Verified Googlebot never gets 401, 402 or 403 from a login wall, paywall or pay-per-crawl setup
  • Every "Soft 404" in the Pages report is improved, merged, or returned as 404/410
  • Every host answers over HTTP/1.1 and HTTP/2, whatever the HTTP/3 setup for browsers
robots.txt
  • Each crawler gets the rules you expect: only its most specific group counts, and groups don't add up
  • No secret or private path is listed in robots.txt; private areas sit behind authentication
  • No unknown lines (Content-Signal, crawl-delay) sit between user-agent lines
  • The homepage with tracking parameters isn't accidentally blocked by a /$ rule
  • Google-Extended is set deliberately, knowing it governs Gemini, not Search
  • The robots.txt report's version history shows no change you didn't make, for example by a CDN
  • Every URL reported as blocked by robots.txt has its redirect chain tested, hop by hop
Crawl efficiency and AI exposure
  • Filter, sort, internal search and action URLs are blocked or normalised; logs show Googlebot spending most requests on pages you want indexed
  • URL variants (case, slashes, parameter order) redirect to one expected URL
  • Load-more and infinite-scroll lists keep a crawlable <a href> to every page of the series
  • The time from publishing a URL to Googlebot's first crawl is tracked, and flat or falling
  • The Generative AI performance report is reviewed before any change to the Search generative AI setting
